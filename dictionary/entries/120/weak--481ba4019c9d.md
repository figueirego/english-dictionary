<a id="r1-s7"></a>

**Source sense R1.S7**

7. Displaying a particular kind of inflection, including&#58;
   1. Regular in inflection, lacking vowel changes and having a past tense marked by /-d-/, /-t-/, or /-ð-/.

      **Usage:** Germanic languages

      **Example:** The verb to walk is weak because it has a past tense of walked and all forms are inflected by adding the typical suffix (ie is regular) to the stem walk- (ie has no vowel changes).

      **Example:** The verb to run is strong, not weak, because the past tense is ran.

      **Example:** The verb to be is neither weak nor strong, instead it is highly irregular in all inflections; its third person present tense is is instead of \*bes (which can even be seen in this very sentence) and its past tense is was or were instead of \*bed or \*beed or anything similar, just to name a few of its inflections.

      **Source topics (extractor hierarchy; not curated):** grammar; human-sciences; linguistics; sciences

      **Source context (verbatim):** of verbs

      **Source antonyms:** strong

10. Displaying a particular kind of inflection, including&#58;
   1. Related to, containing, or being a consonant which is prone to disappearing in some inflections, in most applicable languages including (but not limited to) w and y.

      **Usage:** Semitic languages

      **Example:** In some Semitic languages such as Akkadian, some or all gutturals are weak and often disappear, but in others such as Arabic and Ugaritic, they are strong and never disappear.

      **Example:** The reflexes of the Proto-Semitic verb \*mawut- (“to die”) are often weak due to the presence of w, for example&#58; Arabic مَاتَ (māta), Hebrew מֵת (mēṯ), and Ugaritic 𐎎𐎚 (mt /⁠māta⁠/).

<a id="r1-s12"></a>

**Source sense R1.S12**

12. Of a form in which the accent tends to shift forwards (to the right, in transcription) or did so ancestrally in Proto-Indo-European, relative to the strong stem (which has the leftmost accent allowed).

   **Usage:** Indo-European-studies; especially

   **Example:** The PIE zero grade was most often found in weak stems, such as in \*mn̥téys (“of thought, from thinking”, genitive/ablative singular of \*méntis) and \*mn̥yóh₂er (“I am thinking”). Over time it spread to strong stems, i.e. \*mń̥tis, by processes of analogy and leveling. The full grade was persistent in the first syllable of reduplicated stative verbs&#58; strong \*tetórph₂e (“I am satisfied”), weak \*tetr̥pmé (“we all are satisfied”), though for some verbs such as \*pí-ph₃-e- (“to drink”) there was an unusual i-grade which may have lacked any distinct weak counterpart.

   **Source context (verbatim):** of stems or inflected roots

   **Source antonyms:** strong
