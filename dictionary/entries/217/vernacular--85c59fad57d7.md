### Forms

### Senses

<a id="r1-s1"></a>

**Source sense R1.S1**

1. The language of a people or a national language.

   **Example:** The principal vernacular of the United States is English.

   **Example:** The idea that the Bible should be translated into vernaculars was explosive in medieval society.

   **Source synonyms:** vulgate; vulgar

<a id="r1-s2"></a>

**Source sense R1.S2**

2. Everyday speech or dialect, including colloquialisms, as opposed to standard, literary, liturgical, or scientific idiom.

   **Example:** Street vernacular can be quite different from what is heard elsewhere.

   **Source synonyms:** basilect; demotic

<a id="r1-s3"></a>

**Source sense R1.S3**

3. Language unique to a particular group of people.

   **Example:** For those of a certain age, hiphop vernacular might just as well be a foreign language.

   **Source synonyms:** jargon; argot; dialect; slang

<a id="r1-s4"></a>

**Source sense R1.S4**

4. A language lacking standardization or a written form.

<a id="r1-s5"></a>

**Source sense R1.S5**

5. Indigenous spoken language, as distinct from a literary or liturgical language such as Ecclesiastical Latin.

   **Example:** Vatican II, a church council in the 1960s, allowed the celebration of the mass in the vernacular.

   **Source synonyms:** vulgate; vulgar

<a id="r1-s6"></a>

**Source sense R1.S6**

6. A style of architecture involving local building materials and styles; not imported.

   **Source topics (extractor hierarchy; not curated):** architecture

### Senses

<a id="r2-s1"></a>

**Source sense R2.S1**

1. Of or pertaining to everyday language, as opposed to standard, literary, liturgical, or scientific idiom.

   **Source synonyms:** common; everyday; indigenous; ordinary; vulgar; colloquial; basilectal; demotic
